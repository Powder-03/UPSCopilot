# NCERT Class 12: Indian Society & Social Change in India (Core Conceptual Extract)

## 1. Demographic Structure of Indian Society

### Demographic Transition Theory & India's Profile
- **Stage 1 (Pre-Transition / Primitive)**: High birth rate and high death rate; slow or stagnant population growth (India prior to 1921 — "Year of the Great Divide").
- **Stage 2 (Transitional / Explosion)**: Sharp decline in death rates due to modern medicine, famine control, and sanitation, while birth rates remain stubbornly high; rapid population explosion (India between 1951 and 1991).
- **Stage 3 (Post-Transition / Stabilization)**: Both birth rates and death rates decline; population growth stabilizes. India is currently transitioning through the late second and third stages.
- **Total Fertility Rate (TFR)**:
  - The average number of children born to a woman during her reproductive years.
  - Replacement-level fertility is **2.1** children per woman.
  - **NFHS-5 (2019-21)**: India's national TFR dropped to **2.0**, falling below the replacement level for the first time in history. Wide regional divergence exists: Southern states (e.g., Kerala 1.8, Tamil Nadu 1.8) have stabilized, while Northern states (e.g., Bihar 3.0, UP 2.4) have higher fertility rates.
- **Demographic Dividend**:
  - The economic growth potential that results when a country's working-age population (15–64 years) is larger than the dependent population (under 14 and over 65).
  - India possesses one of the world's youngest populations, with a median age of ~28.4 years and a working-age population exceeding 65%.
  - **Window of Opportunity**: Peaks between 2020 and 2040. Requires massive human capital investments in quality education, skilling, female workforce inclusion, and formal job creation to avoid becoming a "demographic disaster" (unemployment, youth unrest).
- **Dependency Ratio**: Ratio of dependents (children <15 + elderly >60) to the working-age population (15–59). A falling dependency ratio boosts domestic savings and capital accumulation.

## 2. Caste System: Structural Principles & Modern Transformations

### Classical Features vs Modern Realities
- **Varna vs Jati**:
  - *Varna*: Pan-Indian, 4-fold theoretical scriptural classification (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra; with the excluded fifth category of *Avarna* / Untouchables). Rigid, unalterable hierarchy.
  - *Jati*: Locally operating endogamous social groups (over 3,000 jatis across India). Dynamic, region-specific, and contestable.
- **Sociological Principles (Louis Dumont - *Homo Hierarchicus*)**:
  - Caste is founded on the binary opposition of **Purity and Pollution**. Brahmins occupy the apex of ritual purity, while Dalits/Untouchables are assigned tasks deemed permanently ritually polluting (sanitation, leatherwork, disposal of carcasses).
  - Rigid **Endogamy** (marrying strictly within the jati) is the primary mechanism preserving caste purity and property inheritance.
  - Strict rules regarding commensality (dietary restrictions and drinking water sharing) and occupational hereditary monopoly.

### Colonial Impact & Decennial Census
- The **Census of India of 1901** conducted under British Census Commissioner **Herbert Risley** systematically categorized and ranked all jatis in an official hierarchy based on social precedence.
- This institutionalized and hardened caste identities, encouraging jatis to form caste associations (*Sabhas*) and petition the colonial state for higher ranking, transforming flexible social boundaries into rigid legal categories.

### M.N. Srinivas: Foundational Sociological Concepts
1. **Sanskritization**:
   - The process by which a low Hindu caste, tribal, or other group changes its customs, ritual, ideology, and way of life in the direction of a high, frequently "twice-born" (*Dvija*) caste.
   - Low castes adopt vegetarianism, teetotalism, wearing of the sacred thread (*Yajnopavita*), and Sanskrit mantras to claim higher social status.
   - Srinivas noted this leads to **positional change** within the caste system, not **structural change** of the caste system itself.
2. **Westernization**:
   - The changes brought about in Indian society and culture as a result of over 150 years of British rule, encompassing technology, institutions, secular legal systems, rationalism, and lifestyle.
3. **Dominant Caste Concept**:
   - A caste is said to be dominant when it yields decisive political and economic influence in a local area.
   - **Three Essential Determinants**:
     1. Substantial numerical strength in the village/region.
     2. Ownership of sizeable agricultural landholdings (benefited immensely from post-independence Land Reforms and abolition of Zamindari).
     3. Higher access to political power and government employment (e.g., Jats in Haryana/Western UP, Marathas in Maharashtra, Yadavs in Bihar/UP, Lingayats and Vokkaligas in Karnataka, Kammas and Reddys in Andhra Pradesh).

## 3. Tribal Communities: Identity & Integration Debates

### Classification & Distribution
- **Geographical Distribution**:
  - **Central/Middle Indian Belt (~85%)**: Extends from Gujarat/Rajasthan through Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Odisha, to West Bengal. Major tribes: Santhals, Gonds, Bhils, Oraons.
  - **North-Eastern Belt (~11%)**: Nagas, Mizos, Khasis, Garos, Bodos. Characterized by high concentration relative to local state population (e.g., >85% in Mizoram and Meghalaya).
  - **Island/Pocket Communities (<4%)**: Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs, 75 identified groups) such as Sentinelese, Jarawas, Great Andamanese, Birhor, Chenchus. Characterized by pre-agricultural technology, declining/stagnant population, and extreme socio-economic vulnerability.

### Historical Debate: Isolation vs Integration
- **Isolationist Approach (Verrier Elwin)**:
  - Advocated creating protected tribal enclaves ("National Parks") to insulate tribals from predatory moneylenders, traders, and cultural destruction by dominant non-tribal civilization.
- **Assimilationist Approach (G.S. Ghurye)**:
  - Argued that tribals are "backward Hindus" who must be gradually assimilated into mainstream Hindu society and economic structures.
- **Nehruvian Synthesis: The Tribal Panchsheel (1958)**:
  1. People should develop along the lines of their own genius and we should avoid imposing anything upon them.
  2. Tribal rights in land and forests should be respected.
  3. We should try to train and build up a team of their own people for administration and development.
  4. We should not over-administer these areas or overwhelm them with a multiplicity of outside schemes.
  5. We should judge results not by statistics or the amount of money spent, but by the quality of human character that is evolved.

## 4. Social Exclusion & Affirmative Action

### Dalits & Untouchability
- **Three Dimensions of Untouchability**:
  1. **Exclusion**: Physical segregation from village settlements, denial of access to common public wells, temples, and schools.
  2. **Humiliation-Subordination**: Compulsory deferential behavior (walking barefoot, removing headgear, carrying pots to catch spittle).
  3. **Exploitation**: Forged bonded labor (*Begar*), scavenging, and sub-human wages.
- **Legal Safeguards**: Article 17 (Abolition of Untouchability); Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955; Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 (stringent non-bailable arrest and dedicated special courts).

### Other Backward Classes (OBCs) & Reservation Jurisprudence
- **Kaka Kalelkar Commission (First Backward Classes Commission, 1953)**: Identified 2,399 backward castes; recommended using caste as the primary criterion, but was rejected by the central government.
- **B.P. Mandal Commission (Second Backward Classes Commission, 1979)**:
  - Identified 3,743 castes comprising ~52% of India's population as socially and educationally backward.
  - Recommended **27% reservation** in central government jobs and educational institutions.
- **Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992 - 9-Judge Constitution Bench)**:
  - Upheld 27% quota for OBCs.
  - Introduced the **Creamy Layer** exclusion principle for economically advanced OBC members.
  - Imposed a strict **50% ceiling** on total reservations, barring extraordinary situations.
  - Ruled that reservations are confined strictly to initial recruitment and **do not apply to promotions**.

## 5. Secularism, Communalism & Regionalism

### Indian Secularism vs Western Secularism
- **Western Model (Strict Separation of Church and State)**:
  - Mutual non-intervention; strict "wall of separation" between the religious sphere and the political/state sphere. State does not fund or aid any religion.
- **Indian Model (Principled Distance & *Sarva Dharma Sambhava*)**:
  - The state is not anti-religious; it treats all religions with equal respect (*Sarva Dharma Sambhava*).
  - The state maintains "principled distance": It can intervene positively to reform social evils within religion (e.g., Article 17 abolishing untouchability, Article 25(2)(b) opening Hindu temples to all classes, banning Sati/Triple Talaq).
  - Protects religious minorities through fundamental rights: Article 29 (Protection of language, script, culture) and Article 30 (Right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions) with state grants given without discrimination.

### Regionalism & Pluralism
- **Sons of the Soil Doctrine**: Economic competition leading local populations to demand exclusive rights over local employment, educational admissions, and resources (e.g., anti-migrant agitations).
- Reconciled in India through asymmetrical federalism (Articles 371A to 371J, Sixth Schedule Autonomous District Councils) and linguistic states reorganization (State Reorganisation Act, 1956).
