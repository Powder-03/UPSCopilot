# Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)

- **Citation**: (1978) 1 SCC 248; AIR 1978 SC 597
- **Decided On**: January 25, 1978
- **Bench Strength**: 7-Judge Constitution Bench
- **Bench Members**: M.H. Beg C.J., Y.V. Chandrachud, P.N. Bhagwati, V.R. Krishna Iyer, N.L. Untwalia, S. Murtaza Fazal Ali, and P.S. Kailasam JJ.
- **Constitutional Provisions Interpreted**: Article 21, Article 14, Article 19, Passports Act 1967, Overruled *A.K. Gopalan* (1950)

---

## 1. Background & Core Facts
In July 1977, the Regional Passport Officer in New Delhi impounded the passport of journalist Maneka Gandhi under Section 10(3)(c) of the Passports Act, 1967, citing "public interest". When Maneka Gandhi requested the reasons for this impoundment, the government refused to provide them in "the interests of the general public". She filed a writ petition under Article 32 alleging arbitrary executive action without giving her an opportunity to be heard.

---

## 2. Key Constitutional Questions
1. Does the Right to Life and Personal Liberty under Article 21 include the right to travel abroad?
2. Does "procedure established by law" in Article 21 merely require an enacted statute (formal legality), or must the procedure itself be just, fair, and reasonable (substantive due process)?
3. Are Articles 14, 19, and 21 mutually exclusive water-tight compartments (as held in *A.K. Gopalan*), or are they interlinked?
4. Is natural justice (*audi alteram partem*) an integral element of administrative fairness under Article 21?

---

## 3. Majority Ratio Decidendi
1. **Infusion of Substantive Due Process into Article 21**:
   The Supreme Court decisively **overruled *A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* (1950)**. It held that the "procedure established by law" under Article 21 cannot be arbitrary, oppressive, fanciful, or unjust. The procedure must be **"just, fair, and reasonable"**, thereby introducing American substantive "due process" into Indian constitutional law by judicial interpretation.

2. **The Golden Triangle of Fundamental Rights**:
   The Court rejected the "silo" theory of fundamental rights and established that **Articles 14, 19, and 21 form an organic, interrelated trinity (the "Golden Triangle")**. A law depriving personal liberty under Article 21 must also satisfy the non-arbitrariness test of Article 14 and the reasonable restrictions test of Article 19.

3. **Expansive Scope of "Personal Liberty"**:
   "Personal liberty" under Article 21 is not confined to freedom from physical restraint or imprisonment; it is a broad, comprehensive concept encompassing all varieties of rights that go to make up the personal liberties of human beings, including the **right to travel abroad**.

4. **Inseparability of Natural Justice**:
   Principles of natural justice (*audi alteram partem* — the right to be heard) are an implicit part of Article 14 and Article 21. Any administrative order affecting rights without a fair hearing is inherently void.

---

## 4. Key Doctrines & Historical Significance
- Shifted Indian constitutional jurisprudence from **lex** (black-letter statutory law) to **jus** (justice, equity, and fairness).
- Catalyzed the expansion of Article 21 into the powerhouse of Indian civil liberties: right to clean environment, speedy trial, free legal aid, right to livelihood, privacy, and healthcare.
