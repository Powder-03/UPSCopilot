# Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR) v. Union of India (2024)

- **Citation**: 2024 INSC 113; WP (C) No. 880 of 2017
- **Decided On**: February 15, 2024
- **Bench Strength**: 5-Judge Constitution Bench (Unanimous 5-0 decision)
- **Bench Members**: D.Y. Chandrachud C.J., Sanjiv Khanna, B.R. Gavai, J.B. Pardiwala, and Manoj Misra JJ.
- **Constitutional Provisions Interpreted**: Article 19(1)(a), Article 14, Representation of the People Act 1951 (Section 29C), Companies Act 2013 (Section 182), Income Tax Act 1961 (Section 13A)

---

## 1. Background & Core Facts
In 2017–2018, the Union Government introduced the **Electoral Bond Scheme** through the Finance Act, 2017. Under the scheme:
- Any Indian citizen or incorporated company could purchase interest-free bearer promissory notes from the State Bank of India (SBI) in denominations ranging from ₹1,000 to ₹1 crore.
- These bonds could be anonymously donated to registered political parties that secured at least 1% of votes in the previous general or assembly election.
- Amendments removed the statutory ceiling on corporate donations (previously 7.5% of average net profits of the prior 3 years), eliminated the requirement to disclose donor names in political party contribution reports under Section 29C of RPA 1951, and exempted companies from disclosing the identity of recipient political parties in their profit and loss accounts.
- Petitions were filed by ADR, CPI(M), and others challenging the scheme as a violation of voters' right to information and free and fair elections.

---

## 2. Key Constitutional Questions
1. Does the anonymity provided to political contributions under the Electoral Bond Scheme violate the voter's Right to Information under Article 19(1)(a)?
2. Does the unlimited corporate donation provision violate the principle of free and fair elections and Article 14?
3. Can the state cite "curbing black money in politics" or "protecting donor privacy" as a justifiable ground to limit transparency under Article 19(2)?

---

## 3. Majority Ratio Decidendi (Unanimous 5-0 Ruling)
1. **Electoral Bond Scheme Struck Down as Unconstitutional**:
   The Supreme Court struck down the Electoral Bond Scheme, along with the amendments made to Section 29C of the RPA 1951, Section 182 of the Companies Act 2013, and Section 13A of the Income Tax Act 1961, as unconstitutional and violative of **Article 19(1)(a)**.

2. **Voter's Fundamental Right to Information under Article 19(1)(a)**:
   The Court held that **information about funding to political parties is essential for a voter to exercise their democratic franchise meaningfully**. The choice of who to vote for is fundamentally shaped by knowledge of which corporate entities or wealthy individuals are financing which political candidates and parties.

3. **Rejection of Blanket Anonymity**:
   The Court ruled that combating black money in politics is a legitimate aim, but introducing complete donor anonymity is disproportionate and fails the proportionality test. Anonymity allows quid pro quo arrangements, policy capture, and crony capitalism where corporate donations dictate government policy behind closed doors.

4. **Unlimited Corporate Funding is Manifestly Arbitrary**:
   The amendment to Section 182(1) of the Companies Act removing the 7.5% profit cap on corporate donations was declared manifestly arbitrary under Article 14. It permitted loss-making companies and shell companies to be established solely as conduits for routing political donations.

5. **Operative Mandate of Complete Public Disclosure**:
   The Supreme Court directed the State Bank of India (SBI) to immediately halt the sale of electoral bonds, disclose complete transaction details (purchaser names, denominations, date of purchase, and party redemption details with matching alphanumeric codes) to the Election Commission of India (ECI), and ordered the ECI to publish this entire data on its official website for the public.

---

## 4. Key Doctrines & Democratic Insights
- **Money and Speech in Elections**: Money influences politics and creates unequal political access; transparency is the democratic antidote to corporate state capture.
- **Proportionality Test Applied**: The state cannot subvert a fundamental right (transparency of the voter) using a non-constitutional objective (donor privacy).
